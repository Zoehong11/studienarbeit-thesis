\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

NYT Connections asks players to sort sixteen words into four groups of four. Each group carries one of four difficulty levels, ranging from yellow (easiest) to purple (hardest). The puzzle has become a popular benchmark for testing the reasoning abilities of language models. Two challenges make Connections difficult to solve.

The first challenge is lexical or semantical. Solving Connections requires several distinct kinds of language understanding at once. Some groups rest on straightforward synonymy. Others depend on shared themes, wordplay, or knowledge of pop culture and named entities. The taxonomy defined in this thesis can be found in \autoref{tab:connections_relation_taxonomy}. A single puzzle typically mixes several of these relation types, and is deliberately constructed so that some words could plausibly belong to more than one candidate group, an effect commonly referred to as a red herring.

The second challenge is structural. Because a word cannot be reused across groups, correctly identifying even one group depends not only on recognizing a plausible shared category among four words in isolation, but also on the arrangement of the remaining words. A locally plausible grouping can still be wrong if it leaves the remaining words unable to form additional valid groups of four.

In previous studies, the first challenge has been widely discussed and mainly through embedding-based clustering or large language models to propose or judge candidate groupings (see \autoref{sec:prior-work-connections}). An LLM-based approach was explored initially, but a language model is a black box, and it is hard to attribute a specific output to a specific cause. Running a large language model repeatedly across many puzzles and configurations also carries a real cost, whether in paid API usage or in the hardware required to run one locally. This thesis instead takes a different approach. Instead of using LLM to directly suggest groups, several forms of knowledge injections from different sources (see \autoref{sec:exp-sources}) are tested, and utilize a scoring function to suggest candidate groups.

The second challenge, ensuring that all sixteen words are partitioned exclusively, has received comparatively little attention on its own. This thesis treats the puzzle's interactive mechanism (submitting a guess, observing feedback, and revising) as central to address this second challenge. It is inspired by the sequential candidate-elimination strategies used for games such as Wordle and Mastermind, reviewed in \autoref{sec:strategies}. An information-theoretic bound on how much of the puzzle's difficulty feedback alone can resolve is also established, motivating a design in which most of the solving power comes from the scoring function rather than from feedback.

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following empirical contributions, established through the experiments reported in \autoref{chap:experiments}.

First, comparing a greedy selection of groups against an exact global search shows a small improvement in favor of global search.

Second, comparing several sources of semantic enrichment shows that WordNet substantially improves the scoring function, while Wikipedia and Datamuse do not. The improvement is concentrated on puzzles whose categories rest on semantic relations between words.

Third, testing how many pairs of meanings should be averaged when comparing two words shows a further, smaller improvement. Averaging over the two closest pairs performs somewhat better than relying on the single closest pair alone.

Fourth, comparing the one-shot and interactive settings shows that feedback substantially improves solving performance, more than tripling the success rate over the one-shot setting.

Fifth, comparing nine values of the softmax temperature used in the interactive setting shows that it has little effect on the success rate.

\section{Structure}
The structure of this thesis is as follows.
\autoref{chap:related_work} gives an overview of the prior work relevant to solving Connections. \autoref{chap:problem-formulation} formalizes the puzzle as a weighted constraint satisfaction problem. \autoref{chap:method} describes the proposed methodology for solving Connections under both the one-shot and interactive settings. \autoref{chap:experiments} reports the experiments that evaluate this methodology and presents their results. \autoref{chap:conclusion} discusses the results in relation to prior work, concludes the thesis and then outlines directions for future work.
