\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis asked how far Connections can be solved by scoring candidate groups and searching over partitions instead of prompting a language model for the answer, and where the boundary lies between what an algorithm can settle and what has to come from linguistic knowledge. This chapter discusses the results reported in \autoref{chap:experiments}, states the limitations of this evaluation, concludes the thesis and outlines the future work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results are discussed here from three angles: how they compare to the closest prior work, which difficulty levels the improvements concentrate on, and what the interactive feedback contributes.

\subsection*{Comparison with Prior Work}

The closest point of comparison is the heuristic baseline of \citeauthor{loredolopezNYTConnectionsDeceptivelySimple2025}, which also scores candidate groups from embeddings without involving a language model \cite{loredolopezNYTConnectionsDeceptivelySimple2025}. Under their single-attempt configuration it solves 1.0\% of puzzles, against the 7.89\% established in \autoref{sec:exp-aggregation}.

Two differences in setup should be noted. Their evaluation uses 100 puzzles selected around the median difficulty rating, while the 938 puzzles used in this thesis span the full range. Their heuristic solution also uses a smaller embedding model.

Their interactive configurations cannot be compared in the same way, since under No Hints setting and Full Hints setting they report the average proportion of groups found rather than the proportion of puzzles solved.

\subsection*{Limitation of Enrichment}

Every configuration tested improves the easier levels more than the harder ones. Enrichment with WordNet raises the yellow level from 23\% to 38\% and the green level from 15\% to 30\%, while blue moves from 13\% to 17\% and purple from 9\% to 12\%. The interactive setting improves all four levels and the ordering is unchanged.
 
It can be explained by what the resources contain. WordNet organises words by their meanings, so it supplies exactly the kind of knowledge that the semantic categories rely on. It lacks the knowledge of the spelling patterns, homophones, and lists of named entities, which is where the harder levels concentrate. Adding Wikipedia and Datamuse to cover the gap did not work as expected and performed below the baseline as a single source.

\subsection*{What Feedback Contributes}

The interactive setting raises the success rate from 7.89\% to 26.65\% with the same scoring function, so the improvement comes entirely from being able to process responses and revise.
 
This result is consistent with \autoref{chap:problem-formulation}. Feedback can supply at most 6.34 bits against a hypothesis space of 21.3. The remaining information has to come from the scoring function. It contributes a substantial share nonetheless.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

There are four main limitations. First, the development subset of 100 puzzles is contained in the full pool of 938 and is not a held-out set. No parameters are fitted to individual puzzles, so this is not leakage in the usual sense, but results on the two are not independent.

Second, the archive used here ends on 2026-01-06, and puzzles published after that date could serve as a held-out set, but were not available when these experiments were run.

Third, the comparison of knowledge sources in \autoref{sec:exp-sources} was carried out on the development subset only, because Wikipedia and Datamuse require a network request for every word. The differences observed there are large, with WordNet at four times the success rate of Wikipedia, but they have not been confirmed at scale.

Fourth, a single embedding model was used throughout. The relative ordering of the configurations may depend on it, and no comparison across models was attempted.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Much of Connections can be settled without any linguistic knowledge beyond a dictionary. Choosing the four groups together rather than one at a time, expanding each word into its WordNet meanings, and revising the solver's probabilities after each response together take the success rate from 3\% to 26.65\%. Grouping judgement is left to a scoring rule and a search, both of which can be inspected and replaced.

What this approach does not reach is bounded in two ways. Feedback is limited to 6.34 bits per puzzle against a hypothesis space of 21.3, so most of what is needed must already be in the scoring function before the first submission. And the scoring function is limited by the resource it draws on. WordNet describes what words mean, so the levels built on spelling, sound, or named entities remain largely out of reach, which is where the results are weakest.

Both boundaries suggest the directions for future work. The first concerns how the feedback budget is spent. In this thesis, the solver submits the group with the highest marginal probability. An alternative can be submitting the group that yields the most information, even where it is unlikely to be correct in itself. If its three possible responses divide the surviving partitions more evenly, more candidates are eliminated whichever response arrives. This is the criterion used by solvers for Mastermind and Wordle, as described in \autoref{sec:strategies}. The quantities it requires are already established in \autoref{chap:problem-formulation}, and implementing it as a decision rule was not expermimented for lack of time.

The second is about improving the scoring function. WordNet's glosses are fixed and cannot capture a relation that is not meaning-based at all, such as a shared spelling pattern or a named entity. A language model could be used as a further enrichment source instead. It can be prompted to describe a word's possible meanings while shown the full set of sixteen words, rather than serving as the solver itself. Seeing the whole puzzle in this way could let it surface an association that WordNet's context-free glosses miss. Its output would still be embedded and scored by the same mechanism already used for the other resources, rather than trusted as a decision on its own.

At last, the knowledge sources compared here have also only been evaluated at development scale, and confirming them on the full set would settle whether the gaps observed there hold. Moreover, using the puzzles after 2026-01-06 to evaluate the final configuration would offer a more convincing check and result.
